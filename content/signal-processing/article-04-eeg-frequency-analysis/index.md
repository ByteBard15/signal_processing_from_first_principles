## Where are the frequencies?

[Article 3]({{ '/articles/samples-to-spectra/' | relative_url }}) ended with a finite record whose construction we knew. If

$$
x[n]=\sin\left(2\pi f_1n/f_s\right)+A\sin\left(2\pi f_2n/f_s\right),
$$

then its peaks, leakage and aliases can be predicted from $f_1$, $f_2$, $f_s$, $N$, and the observation window. Now suppose the input is a scalp electroencephalogram. Its underlying components are not printed beside the samples. The waveform is irregular, its amplitude changes, contamination overlaps the frequencies of interest, and sixty-four channels observe related but unequal voltage differences.

The question is no longer merely “what does the FFT return?” It is:

> **How do our frequency-analysis tools behave when the signal is noisy, nonstationary, multichannel, and biological?**

{% include interactive-figure.html id="fig-eeg-trace" experiment="eegTrace" title="Known tones beside an unknown mixture" description="Compare a designed two-tone signal with a conditioned C3 EEG epoch in time and frequency." caption="Both records have Fourier coordinates. Only the synthetic record comes with a known component list. The EEG example is Subject S001, Run 04 of PhysioNet EEGMMIDB; it is illustrative, not representative of every subject." %}

The EEG spectrum is not useless because it lacks two perfect lines. It asks for a different kind of interpretation: how power is distributed, how stable that estimate is, when the distribution changes, and whether an observed difference survives reasonable analysis choices.

## What does one EEG channel measure?

Scalp EEG records voltage **differences**. An electrode does not read a neuron in isolation; it participates in a measurement of electrical fields generated by the combined activity of neuronal populations, volume-conducted through tissue to the scalp. The acquisition reference matters because changing the reference changes every channel. Typical plotted values are on a microvolt scale, but amplitude depends on montage, hardware, preprocessing, and the recording itself.

The international electrode labels are spatial names. The letter gives an approximate region—`Fp` frontopolar, `F` frontal, `C` central, `P` parietal, `O` occipital—while odd numbers lie on the left, even numbers on the right, and `z` lies near the midline. Thus C3 and C4 are left and right central scalp locations; Cz lies between them. This is useful spatial context, not precise anatomical localization.

For $C$ channels and $N$ sample instants, write the recording as

$$
\mathbf X\in\mathbb R^{C\times N}.
$$

Every row is a time series, but the rows are neither independent sensors of isolated sources nor interchangeable repetitions.

## The recording used in the experiments

The examples use a compact derivative of the open [PhysioNet EEG Motor Movement/Imagery Dataset](https://doi.org/10.13026/C28G6P). The full dataset contains 64-channel EDF+ recordings from 109 volunteers, sampled at 160 samples/s with BCI2000. We retain only Subject S001, motor-imagery Run 04, eight channels, the event annotations, and four-second labelled intervals. In this run, `T0` means rest, `T1` left-fist imagery, and `T2` right-fist imagery. Those mappings must not be copied blindly to other run types. [R8](#r8) [R9](#r9)

The processed example contains physical values decoded from the EDF calibration and no hidden filtering. The reproducible converter is [`scripts/prepare-eeg-example.mjs`](https://github.com/{{ site.repository }}/blob/main/scripts/prepare-eeg-example.mjs); the full EDF is not bundled. This is a manageable teaching subset, not a claim that Subject S001 summarizes the population.

## The signal is never just “the brain”

A useful measurement model is

$$
x_{\rm measured}[n]
=x_{\rm neural}[n]+x_{\rm interference}[n]
+x_{\rm artifact}[n]+\eta[n].
$$

Baseline drift contributes very slow variation. Power-line coupling contributes a narrowband component near 50 or 60 Hz. Eye blinks can create large low-frequency transients. Muscle activity can add broadband, often higher-frequency energy. Sensors and electronics add noise. These categories overlap spectrally, so frequency alone does not reveal origin.

{% include interactive-figure.html id="fig-eeg-artifacts" experiment="eegArtifacts" title="Compose an observed EEG-like measurement" description="Toggle drift, 60 Hz interference, a blink, muscle-like activity and measurement noise around an illustrative neural component." caption="Because the construction is synthetic, each term is known. A real electrode supplies only their sum; filtering cannot generally recover the hidden terms exactly." %}

The toggle experiment is deliberately honest about what it proves. It demonstrates how different causes appear in one record, but it does not validate an artifact-removal algorithm. A peak at 10 Hz is not automatically neural, and energy near 60 Hz is not automatically only line interference.

## Preprocessing is a signal-processing decision

Conditioning should begin with a question, not a standard sequence of buttons:

- Detrending asks whether an offset or linear drift should influence this interval.
- A high-pass filter attenuates slow variation, including any genuine slow component.
- A low-pass filter attenuates rapid variation, including genuine activity above its cutoff.
- A notch attenuates a narrow band around line frequency, whether its content is interference or not.
- Re-referencing replaces each voltage difference with another set of voltage differences; it does not reveal an absolute potential.

The experiment detrends and then uses forward–backward second-order filter sections. Applying a section in both directions cancels its phase delay and squares its magnitude response. That choice is appropriate for offline illustration, not causal real-time processing, and it can create edge transients.

{% include interactive-figure.html id="fig-eeg-preprocessing" experiment="eegPreprocess" title="Every filter changes the evidence" description="Select a channel and adjust high-pass, low-pass and 60 Hz notch choices while inspecting the trace, PSD and actual combined response." caption="The before/after plots use the same four-second interval. A cleaner-looking trace is not proof of cleaner neural activity; the response panel states exactly what the chosen cascade attenuates." %}

An aggressive cutoff can manufacture a persuasive story by removing inconvenient content. We will therefore state the defaults—linear detrend, 1–40 Hz conditioning, no notch—and later test whether conclusions depend strongly on them.

## Epochs: when are we analysing?

A two-minute run is not one homogeneous event. Its annotations alternate rest and imagery intervals. Computing one spectrum over the whole run would mix conditions and suppress the question we actually want to ask.

An **epoch** is a selected interval associated with an event or condition. Here, each retained epoch begins at a PhysioNet annotation and lasts four seconds. This makes the analysis unit explicit:

$$
x_{e,c}[n],\qquad e=1,\ldots,E,\quad c=1,\ldots,C.
$$

{% include interactive-figure.html id="fig-eeg-epochs" experiment="eegEpochs" title="Select evidence by event, not appearance" description="Move through the annotated Run 04 timeline and inspect the same four-second rule at different channels and conditions." caption="No epoch is hand-cropped around an attractive waveform. Event codes determine the intervals before their spectra are inspected." %}

Epoching does not make the signal stationary. It limits the claim: a spectral estimate now describes this defined interval under this event label.

## The FFT is not yet a power spectral estimate

For one epoch, the FFT provides the same complex DFT coefficients derived in Part III. An amplitude spectrum rescales their magnitudes to estimate sinusoidal amplitude. A **power spectrum** instead assigns squared signal units to discrete frequency components. A **power spectral density** assigns power per unit frequency, here $\mu\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}$.

Those quantities cannot share labels casually. For a window $w[n]$, our one-sided modified periodogram is

$$
\widehat S_{xx}[k]
=\frac{q_k}{f_s\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w^2[n]}
\left\lvert\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]w[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}\right\rvert^2,
$$

where $q_k=2$ for interior positive-frequency bins and $q_k=1$ at DC and, for even $N$, Nyquist. Multiplying each density value by $\Delta f=f_s/N$ and summing approximates the epoch's mean-square voltage after window-dependent estimation effects.

{% include interactive-figure.html id="fig-eeg-psd" experiment="eegPsd" title="One epoch, three different questions" description="Inspect its time trace, amplitude spectrum, normalized periodogram and the periodograms of other rest epochs." caption="The FFT is the computation inside both spectral displays. Their normalization and units determine whether they describe amplitude or power density." %}

The separate rest periodograms vary substantially. This is not an FFT failure. The epochs are different realizations of a variable process, and a raw periodogram has high variance.

## Welch asks for a typical spectrum

How can we stabilize a power estimate without pretending that repeated intervals are identical? Welch's construction reuses operations already established: [R5](#r5)

1. Split a record into $K$ possibly overlapping segments of length $L$.
2. Multiply each segment by a window.
3. compute its correctly normalized modified periodogram.
4. Average at each frequency.

If the $r$th segment begins at $rD$, then

$$
\widehat S_{xx}^{\rm Welch}[k]
=\frac1K\sum_{r=0}^{K-1}
\frac{q_k\left\lvert X_r[k]\right\rvert^2}
{f_s\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}w^2[n]},
$$

with

$$
X_r[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}x[n+rD]w[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/L}.
$$

Averaging reduces the erratic variation of individual estimates. It does not create additional independent measurements: overlapping segments share samples and are correlated.

{% include interactive-figure.html id="fig-eeg-welch" experiment="eegWelch" title="Build Welch's estimate instead of calling it" description="Change segment length, overlap and window; inspect the selected segments, their individual modified periodograms and their average." caption="Long segments give finer bin spacing but fewer averages. Short segments give more averages and a smoother estimate but coarser frequency resolution." %}

The trade-off is the same duration law from Part III. A segment of duration $T_L=L/f_s$ has spacing $\Delta f=f_s/L=1/T_L$. Welch exchanges some frequency detail for lower estimator variance.

## Frequency bands are summaries, not laws

Only after defining PSD can we sensibly summarize its area. This article uses approximate intervals delta 1–4 Hz, theta 4–8 Hz, alpha 8–13 Hz, beta 13–30 Hz, and gamma 30–45 Hz. Boundaries differ across studies, age groups, tasks, and analysis conventions. A label is not a universal mapping from frequency to mental state.

For a band $B=[f_a,f_b]$, band power is the PSD integral

$$
P_B=\int_{f_a}^{f_b}S_{xx}(f)\,df,
$$

approximated here by trapezoidal integration over the frequency bins. Its unit is $\mu\text{V}^2$, not $\mu\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}$, because frequency has been integrated out.

## From band power to a real question

Motor-imagery work often examines sensorimotor rhythms near central electrodes, including approximate mu/alpha and beta ranges. Prior studies motivate that question, but they do not guarantee the same effect in each subject, epoch, channel, or preprocessing pipeline. [R10](#r10)

Our bounded question is:

> **For this recording, do retained sensorimotor channels show systematic spectral-power differences between labelled rest and motor-imagery epochs?**

We compare every retained epoch under the same rules rather than selecting one attractive trace.

{% include interactive-figure.html id="fig-eeg-conditions" experiment="eegConditions" title="Compare conditions with their variability visible" description="Choose a channel and left- or right-fist imagery; compare condition means and epoch-wise interquartile ranges." caption="Mean curves answer a condition-level question for this run. Quartile curves show how much individual epochs vary and discourage conclusions from one handpicked interval." %}

The graph reports what we observe under stated choices. A difference may suggest a task-related spectral change; it does not establish neural source, causal mechanism, population generality, or clinical meaning.

## Multichannel data add a spatial question

A single trace discards the defining structure of EEG: channels sample different scalp locations simultaneously. For each channel $c$, we may estimate $S_{cc}(f)$ and band power, but nearby channels can share activity through volume conduction and the common reference.

{% include interactive-figure.html id="fig-eeg-channels" experiment="eegChannels" title="One event, several scalp measurements" description="Select an electrode and condition; compare simultaneous traces, its mean PSD, and 8–13 Hz power across the retained channel subset." caption="The reduced set emphasizes central locations and includes Fp1 as a frontal contrast. It is a channel explorer, not a source-localization or scalp-topography model." %}

Spatial variation matters, but a high value at C3 does not prove that the generator lies directly beneath C3. A scalp map would interpolate electrode measurements; an anatomical source claim would require a separate forward/inverse model and its assumptions.

## A global spectrum forgets when

Suppose a four-second record contains strong 10 Hz activity for one second and strong 20 Hz activity later. Its whole-record PSD can show both. It cannot say which occurred first. EEG makes this limitation consequential because its spectral statistics can change within a trial.

This is the unresolved question from Part III in biological form:

> **What if spectral content changes faster than the observation interval used for one spectrum?**

## The STFT is a moving local spectrum

Begin with the operation, not the name. Select a short window around one time, compute the spectrum of those weighted samples, move the window, and repeat. The Short-Time Fourier Transform records the complex result at frame $m$ and frequency bin $k$:

$$
X[m,k]=\sum_n x[n]w[n-mD]e^{-j2\pi kn/L},
$$

where $L$ is the window length and $D$ the hop. A spectrogram displays a power-normalized version of $\lvert X[m,k]\rvert^2$. [R12](#r12)

{% include interactive-figure.html id="fig-eeg-stft-build" experiment="eegStftBuild" title="Watch one spectrogram column being made" description="Move a Hann window across an EEG epoch; connect the selected samples to their local periodogram and the accumulated time-frequency image." caption="Every column is a familiar windowed power estimate. The second axis appears because the operation is repeated at successive times." %}

The signal inside each window is not guaranteed stationary. The local interpretation is an approximation whose credibility depends on how rapidly the process changes relative to $L/f_s$.

## Reading a spectrogram without hiding its scale

The spectrogram uses time on the horizontal axis, frequency on the vertical axis, and PSD colour in dB relative to $1\,\mu\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}$. The colour limits remain fixed while controls change; automatic rescaling would make unrelated panels look artificially similar.

{% include interactive-figure.html id="fig-eeg-spectrogram" experiment="eegSpectrogram" title="Trade frequency localization for time localization" description="Change condition, channel, window duration and overlap while comparing one global periodogram with local power through time." caption="A short window places changes more tightly in time but broadens spectral structure. A long window narrows frequency spacing while averaging over a longer interval. Overlap makes more columns, not more intrinsic resolution." %}

The central trade-off remains

$$
\Delta f\approx\frac1{T_{\rm window}}.
$$

There is no window length that simultaneously gives arbitrarily sharp time and frequency localization.

## Nonstationary does not mean merely noisy

A stationary random process can produce an irregular trace while keeping the same statistical structure over time. A nonstationary signal changes that structure—its mean, variance, spectrum, or other statistics depend on time. Noise describes unpredictability; nonstationarity describes change.

EEG can contain both. Welch averaging is useful when a typical spectrum is the target and local differences may be sacrificed. The STFT is useful when those local differences are the target. Neither method makes stationarity true; each chooses a time scale on which an approximation is made.

## Event-related spectral change

One way to align trials is to express task-band power relative to a baseline. We use the explicit convention

$$
R_B(t)=100\%\times
\frac{P_{B,\rm task}(t)-\overline P_{B,\rm rest}}
{\overline P_{B,\rm rest}}.
$$

Negative changes in an oscillatory band are commonly described as event-related desynchronization (ERD), and positive changes as event-related synchronization (ERS). These names refer to relative band-power change, not to neurons literally becoming disconnected or synchronized in a complete physiological sense. Definitions and baseline conventions must be reported. [R11](#r11)

## Does the observation survive our choices?

A result is not robust merely because a plot contains two separated curves. We should vary reasonable analysis decisions without searching for the combination that looks most impressive.

{% include interactive-figure.html id="fig-eeg-robustness" experiment="eegRobustness" title="Try to make the conclusion move" description="Change channel, high-pass cutoff, Welch segment length, overlap and window; compare condition means and the cross-channel 8–13 Hz contrast." caption="Stable qualitative structure is more persuasive than one optimized display. Sign changes and large swings are findings about sensitivity, not inconveniences to hide." %}

Several effects should be expected:

- Segment length changes frequency spacing and the number of averages.
- Window choice changes main-lobe width, sidelobes and the effective weighting of samples.
- Overlap changes how many correlated estimates enter the average.
- Filter cutoffs change the signal before estimation.
- Channel selection changes the scalp measurement being summarized.
- Epoch selection changes which realization supports the condition mean.

This experiment does not exhaust robustness. A fuller study would compare subjects, runs, references, artifact policies, epoch definitions and statistical models. It does establish the habit: parameter sensitivity belongs in the result.

## What have we actually learned?

The path was

<div class="pipeline pipeline--article" role="img" aria-label="Raw multichannel EEG proceeds through conditioning, labelled epochs, periodograms, Welch power spectral density, channel and condition comparisons, short-time spectra, and robustness checks.">
  <strong>raw EEG</strong><b>→</b><strong>conditioning</strong><b>→</b><strong>epochs</strong><b>→</b><strong>periodogram</strong><b>→</b><strong>Welch PSD</strong><b>→</b><strong>channels and conditions</strong><b>→</b><strong>STFT</strong><b>→</b><strong>robustness</strong>
</div>

The mathematics from Parts I–III survived, but its role changed:

1. Real measurements do not generally separate into isolated visible Fourier peaks.
2. Observation duration, windowing and preprocessing materially change spectral estimates.
3. PSD describes power per frequency; it is not squared FFT magnitude with an arbitrary label.
4. Periodograms vary. Welch reduces variance by averaging at a cost in local frequency detail.
5. Band power is an integral over declared, non-universal boundaries.
6. EEG is multichannel, so temporal and spatial measurement choices coexist.
7. A global spectrum loses timing; the STFT restores a local time-frequency description with a resolution trade-off.
8. Nonstationarity differs from noise.
9. A conclusion that depends on one convenient parameter setting is part of the uncertainty, not yet a robust finding.

We did not identify a neurological condition. We asked what frequency analysis means when the input is a real biological measurement, and what conclusions remain after the assumptions are made visible.

## A wider research direction

EEG offers millisecond-scale temporal sampling but limited direct spatial localization. Other modalities, including fMRI, measure different physical processes with very different spatial and temporal properties. That motivates a broader question: **can complementary neural measurements be combined into more robust representations of changing brain states?**

Multimodal EEG–fMRI, computational neuroimaging and neural signal fusion are research directions, not automatic next steps from this one example. The next planned article in this series remains the image-to-sound investigation; multimodal neural measurement belongs to a separate future program.
